Japanese
culture

More than two thousand years of history, religious syncretism, unique life philosophies, and an aesthetic that has conquered the world. Japan does not export only entertainment — it exports a different way of seeing existence.

Japanese thought

Philosophies that guide life

Japanese culture has its own philosophical vocabulary — words without exact translation — that define ways of feeling, living, and relating to the world that do not exist in other languages. They are not abstract concepts: they are everyday practices that shape society, art, and ultimately anime.[1]

Many of these concepts have their origin in Zen Buddhism and Shintoism, but have evolved to become part of the Japanese collective unconscious, permeating everything from architecture to the way of serving a cup of tea.

Ikigai places at the centre each person's reason for being.[2]

侘寂

Wabi-Sabi

wabi-sabi

Finding beauty in imperfection, incompleteness, and impermanence. A broken bowl repaired with gold is more beautiful than an intact one — its scars tell its story. The aesthetic of wabi‑sabi permeates architecture, Zen gardens, and Japanese visual arts.[3]

In anime: the melancholy of Ghibli landscapes, the imperfect characters who grow without ever being fully resolved.

物哀

Mono no Aware

mono no aware — "the empathy towards things"

The bittersweet awareness that everything is ephemeral. Coined in the Heian period and later taken up by scholar Motoori Norinaga in the 18th century. Appreciating a cherry blossom knowing it will fall in a few days is the purest experience of mono no aware.[4]

In anime: the end of Clannad, the flashbacks in Violet Evergarden, the Hanami in the first episode of Fruits Basket.

改善

Kaizen

kaizen — "continuous improvement"

The philosophy of constant small improvements. Not great revolutions but daily micro‑changes that, accumulated, produce extraordinary transformations. Toyota, Sony, and Walt Disney Company are global examples of its application.[5]

In anime: the training of Goku, Rock Lee, or Izuku Midoriya — overcoming is never a leap, always a process.

我慢

Gaman

gaman — "to endure with dignity"

The ability to bear the unbearable with patience and without outward complaint. Linked to Zen Buddhism, gaman is Japanese stoicism: not denying pain, but going through it silently without losing composure.[1]

In anime: Spike Spiegel in Cowboy Bebop, Itachi Uchiha in Naruto, the characters of Vinland Saga facing tragedy.

金繕

Kintsukuroi

kintsukuroi — "to repair with gold"

The art of repairing broken ceramics with gold, silver, or platinum lacquer. The repaired piece does not hide its fractures — it celebrates them. A powerful metaphor: breaks are part of an object's (or a person's) history and make it more valuable.[1]

In anime: characters who grow through trauma without erasing it — Nana Osaki, Rei Kiriyama, Ken Kaneki.

Torii (鳥居) mark the threshold between the profane and the sacred.

Worldview

Faith without forced choice: religious syncretism

Japan has a unique relationship with religion. Most Japanese people do not declare themselves devotees of any particular faith, and yet religion is present on every corner of their daily lives. This phenomenon is called syncretism: the harmonious coexistence of multiple belief systems without contradiction.[6]

It is common for the same family to celebrate New Year at a Shinto shrine, marry in a Shinto or Christian ceremony, and bury their dead with Buddhist rites — without perceiving any inconsistency.

Shinto (神道, "way of the gods") is Japan's native religion, the only one that existed before the arrival of Buddhism. It is polytheistic: it venerates kami, spirits that inhabit every natural element — mountains, trees, rivers, wind. Its pillars are ritual purification, reverence for nature, family, and seasonal festivals.[6]

Jinja (神社, Shinto shrines) are the sacred spaces where kami receive veneration. Japan has more than 80,000 registered shrines — more than convenience stores (konbini). The iconic torii arch always marks the entrance to the sacred space.

Buddhism arrived in Japan from Korea in the 6th century and merged with Shinto so deeply that both are sometimes considered a single faith. The current that has had the greatest impact on Japanese culture is Zen Buddhism (禅), which prioritises direct meditation over the study of texts.[6]

Zen directly influenced Japanese arts: the tea ceremony (chanoyu), calligraphy, gravel gardens (karesansui), Noh theatre, and minimalist architecture. The idea of finding the infinite in the small — a cup, a brushstroke, a stone — is profoundly Zen.

Japanese folklore is a universe of its own: thousands of supernatural creatures called yōkai (妖怪) populate Japanese mythology. They are not simply monsters — they are beings with complex personalities, sometimes benevolent, sometimes mischievous, sometimes terrifying. Each region of Japan has its particular yōkai.[7]

Nature kami, wandering spirits called yūrei, magical foxes (kitsune), mischievous raccoons (tanuki), and mountain tengu are just some of the entities that populate the Japanese imagination — and appear in hundreds of anime, from Natsume's Book of Friends to Jujutsu Kaisen or Spirited Away.

Matsuri 祭り

The calendar of celebrations

Matsuri are not just festivals — they are the pulse of the community. Each season has its own celebrations, each linked to nature, spirituality, and local identity.[8]

🌸

Spring

春 — Haru
🌸 Mar–Apr

Hanami 花見

Cherry blossom viewing. Families and friends gather under the sakura trees for picnics and to reflect on the fleeting nature of life — the quintessence of mono no aware.

🎏 May

Kodomo no Hi 子供の日

Children's Day. Cloth carp streamers (koinobori) are hung to symbolize perseverance, strength, and success for the family's youth.

🎆

Summer

夏 — Natsu
⭐ Jul 7

Tanabata 七夕

Star Festival. Inspired by the legend of Orihime and Hikoboshi, two lovers separated by the Milky Way who meet once a year. Wishes are written on paper strips and hung on bamboo.

🏮 Aug

Obon お盆

Festival to honor the spirits of ancestors. Families return to their hometowns, lanterns are lit on rivers, and the bon odori dance is performed in the streets.

🍂

Autumn

秋 — Aki
🍁 Oct–Nov

Momiji-gari 紅葉狩り

The autumn version of Hanami: viewing the red and gold foliage of maple trees. In Japan, appreciating the change of seasons is a cultural practice, not just a tourist activity.

👘 Nov

Shichi-Go-San 七五三

A rite of passage for children aged 3, 5, and 7 who visit Shinto shrines dressed in kimono. It celebrates their growth and asks for divine protection.

❄️

Winter

冬 — Fuyu
🎍 Jan 1

Shōgatsu お正月

Japanese New Year is the most important celebration on the calendar. Families perform hatsumōde (the first shrine visit), eat osechi ryōri, and send nengajō cards.

👹 Feb

Setsubun 節分

The last day of winter according to the lunar calendar. Soybeans are thrown to drive away demons (oni) and bring good luck into the home.

Washoku 和食

Gastronomy as ritual

Japanese cuisine (washoku) was declared an Intangible Cultural Heritage by UNESCO in 2013. It is not just food: it is an aesthetic and philosophical practice where presentation, seasonality of ingredients, and respect for the diner are as important as flavour.[6]

The ritual of itadakimasu (いただきます) before every meal — gratitude to everyone who made the dish possible, from the farmer to nature — encapsulates the Japanese philosophy of gratitude and interconnectedness.

Japan has more Michelin‑starred restaurants than any other country in the world, and Japanese cuisine boasts over 150,000 restaurants outside Japan.[9]

ラーメン

Ramen

Noodle soup in pork, chicken, miso, or soy broth. Each region has its variant: Tokyo shoyu, Hokkaido miso, Fukuoka tonkotsu. Featured in anime such as Naruto and Food Wars.

寿司

Sushi

Japan's gastronomic ambassador. Omakase (entrusting yourself to the chef) is its highest form: an experience of mutual respect between cook and diner.

茶道

Chanoyu (Tea)

The tea ceremony is meditation in motion: every gesture has meaning. Green tea (matcha) is the thread linking Zen Buddhism and wabi‑sabi aesthetics.

和菓子

Wagashi

Traditional sweets that change with the seasons. Anmitsu, mochi, and nerikiri are edible works of art with an ephemeral existence — pure mono no aware.

おにぎり

Onigiri

The Japanese "sandwich". Rice ball with filling wrapped in seaweed. Ubiquitous in anime — an onigiri is always a symbol of care and home.

Cool Japan 🌐

Anime as cultural ambassador

Anime does not only entertain — it transmits. Generations of viewers around the world have learned about Hanami, bushidō, Shinto, and the philosophy of effort through animated stories.

"Anime has played a role of cultural dissemination in its internationalisation, serving as a bridge that awakens curiosity and interest in learning more about Japan."

— Mundana Magazine, 2025[1]

⛩️

Shinto and sacred nature

The Shinto worldview — where natural elements are imbued with divinity — reaches millions of Western viewers through anime, creating an understanding of the relationship with nature that radically differs from the Western perspective of exploitation.[1]

My Neighbor Totoro, Spirited Away, Princess Mononoke — Studio Ghibli

⚔️

Bushidō: the warrior's code

The values of bushidō — loyalty, honour, effort, self‑determination — permeated the ethics of millions through shōnen anime. The master‑disciple relationship and self‑improvement are deeply linked to this samurai tradition.[1]

Naruto, Demon Slayer, Rurouni Kenshin, Samurai Champloo

🎆

Festivals as a cultural window

Matsuri, Golden Week, summer fireworks, Tanabata, and Obon have become recognisable worldwide thanks to anime, which integrates them organically into their stories.[1]

Fruits Basket, Your Name, AnoHana, Toradora

🍜

Gastronomy as protagonist

Anime such as Food Wars, Sweetness and Lightning, or Isekai Izakaya have turned Japanese cuisine into a global object of desire, boosting interest in washoku and the tea ceremony in markets that previously knew nothing about them.

Food Wars, Sweetness and Lightning, Isekai Izakaya Nobu

🤝

Social values: group over individual

Anime reflects wa (和, harmony) as a core value of Japanese society: the importance of the group, individual sacrifice for collective well‑being, and the weight of social obligations (giri) that shape every decision.

Haikyuu!, My Hero Academia, Saikano, Sword Art Online
