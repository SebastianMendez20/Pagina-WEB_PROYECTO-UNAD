Before 1917 Precursors

Roots: from emakimono to the frame

Japanese narrative art and early Western animations

To understand anime, one must go back much further than most imagine. The Japanese narrative tradition has a millenary visual history: since the 12th century, emakimono —illustrated scrolls unrolled from right to left— narrated legends and battles as if they were moving panoramas. The puppets of the bunraku theater, the kagee shadow art, and the ukiyo-e prints of the Edo period (1603–1868) are considered visual ancestors of anime characters.[1]

In the West, the first documented animated film was Humorous Phases of Funny Faces (1906), by American producer James Stuart Blackton. In 1908, French cartoonist Émile Cohl produced Fantasmagorie. These works reached Japanese theaters around 1910, and between 1914 and 1917 about 93 foreign animated films were shown in Japan, with American ones being the most popular.[2] Their impact was immediate: Japanese producers began to consider creating their own animation.

There is also an earlier work of unknown authorship: Katsudō Shashin (活動写真), a fragment of barely 3–4 seconds discovered in Kyoto in 2005 and dated between 1907 and 1911. Although the Asahi Shimbun warns that calling it "animation" in the contemporary sense may be controversial, it is the oldest known manifestation of moving images produced in Japan.[3]

1917 – 1945 Experimental Era

The three pioneers and the dark age

Birth of the medium, the Great Earthquake, and war propaganda

The year 1917 marks the formal start of Japanese animation with the coincidence of three creators working independently and without communication with each other. None had documentation on animation techniques, so each experimented on their own, through trial and error.[2]

January 1917

Ōten Shimokawa

Created Imokawa Mukuzō Genkanban no Maki, considered the first publicly released Japanese animated short. He drew figures on a chalkboard. He made five shorts, but the visual strain damaged his eyesight and he returned to manga.

May 1917

Seitarō Kitayama

Produced Saru Kani Gassen (The Battle of the Monkey and the Crab), based on a Japanese folk tale. He made up to ten films that year and exported a Momotarō adaptation to France, the first anime to reach the West.

June 1917

Jun'ichi Kōuchi

Released Namakura Gatana (The Dull Sword), a 4-minute short about a samurai who buys a blunt katana. It is the oldest surviving work; it was rediscovered in 2008 in an antique shop in Osaka.

The Great Kantō Earthquake (1923)

The early development of anime suffered a devastating blow when the Great Kantō Earthquake destroyed studios and warehouses in Tokyo. Countless pioneering works were lost forever in the fires, leaving a historical void that film researchers are still trying to fill.[1] Kitayama, whose studio was destroyed, moved to Osaka and abandoned animation to dedicate himself to documentaries.

Anime at the service of war (1930–1945)

As Japan entered its militaristic period, the imperial government recognized the power of animation as a propaganda tool. The most significant work of this period was Momotarō: Umi no Shinpei (Momotarō, Sacred Sailors, 1945), the first Japanese animated feature film, financed by the Imperial Navy. Ironically, many of the animators who worked on these wartime productions would later build the creative postwar anime industry.[4]

1948 – 1962 Toei Dōga Era

The dream of being the Disney of the East

Toei Animation and the first high-quality feature films

With Japan rebuilding after the war, the cultural industry took on a vital role. In 1948, Nihon Dōga Eigasha was founded, which in 1956 would become Toei Dōga (today Toei Animation) with a declared ambition: to become the "Disney of the East". Unlike the television model that would come later, Toei bet on high-budget feature films with fluid and detailed animation.[4]

In 1958 they released Hakujaden (The Tale of the White Serpent), the first Japanese color animated feature, based on a Chinese legend. The film demonstrated that Japan could produce world-class animation and had an unexpected consequence: a young man named Hayao Miyazaki saw it in theaters and was so impressed that he decided to dedicate his life to animation.

In Toei's hallways, Isao Takahata also trained. These two names, who decades later would found Studio Ghibli, learned at Toei the philosophy of "quality over quantity" that would mark their entire careers.

1963 – 1979 TV Revolution

Osamu Tezuka and the birth of television anime

Astro Boy, limited animation, and the rise of Mecha

Osamu Tezuka (1928–1989), known in Japan as the "God of Manga" (漫画の神様), was the artist who transformed anime from a niche cinematic entertainment into a mass‑reach television industry. His influence was so profound that it is impossible to imagine modern anime without him.[5]

"January 1, 1963, was the inaugural broadcast of Astro Boy. It was the first Japanese TV animated program produced as a series. Weekly 30‑minute animated TV series are commonplace today, and Astro Boy was the first of its kind in the world."

— Osamu Tezuka, quoted in Google Arts & Culture / Tezuka Productions

Limited animation: necessity as virtue

To produce 30 minutes of animation per week —unheard of at the time— Tezuka and his team at Mushi Production developed limited animation: a technique that reduced the number of frames per second and reused backgrounds and sequences. Instead of fluid movements, they opted for exaggerated facial expressions, dynamic camera movements, and prolonged static shots that conveyed emotion without needing to redraw.[6]

What was born from budget constraints became one of the most recognizable traits of anime. The first episode achieved a 27.4% audience share; the historical peak was 40.7%, recorded on August 29, 1964.[7]

Key works of the period (1963–1979)

1963
Astro Boy (Tetsuwan Atomu)

First weekly 30‑minute anime series. Combined science fiction with moral dilemmas about coexistence between humans and robots. It was exported to the US, China, and European countries.[7]

1963
Kimba the White Lion (Jungle Taitei)

Tezuka's second major series. It was the first TV anime series broadcast in color in Japan and paved the way for systematic export to the West.

1972
Mazinger Z

Created by Go Nagai, it popularized the mecha genre (giant robots piloted by humans), one of the pillars of anime that would define decades of production.

1979
Mobile Suit Gundam

Introduced a more realistic and political approach to the mecha genre, with morally complex interstellar wars. It generated an expanded universe that remains active today.

1980 – 1999 Golden Age

The Golden Age: Akira, Ghibli, and global conquest

OVAs, cyberpunk, Dragon Ball, and legitimization as art

In the 1980s, Japan experienced an unprecedented economic bubble. The surplus of capital spilled over into the cultural industry and allowed a creative freedom that had not existed before. The OVA (Original Video Animation) format was born: direct‑to‑VHS releases that bypassed television censorship and the pressure for mass box‑office success. OVAs enabled adult, experimental, violent, and erotic anime that would define a generation of cult following.[4]

Akira (1988): the introduction to the world

The technical culmination of this era was Akira (1988), by Katsuhiro Otomo, a dystopian science‑fiction film set in Neo‑Tokyo. With an extraordinary budget for the time, it used 327 different colors —50 created specifically for the film— and abandoned Tezuka's limited animation to return to total fluidity. It also applied the pre‑scoring technique: actors recorded their voices before animation, and characters synced their lips to the audio, unheard of in Japan until then.[4]

Studio Ghibli: anime as art cinema

In 1985, Hayao Miyazaki and Isao Takahata founded Studio Ghibli, taking the quality philosophy both had learned at Toei to its highest expression. Their films combined exquisite animation with mature narratives about nature, war, childhood, and identity. My Neighbor Totoro (1988) and Grave of the Fireflies (1988) —released the same year— demonstrated that anime could be devastatingly beautiful and emotionally devastating at the same time.[8]

The definitive milestone of international legitimization came in 2003, when Spirited Away (Sen to Chihiro no Kamikakushi, 2001) won the Oscar for Best Animated Feature, becoming the first non‑English‑language film to win that award in that category.

Global expansion (1986–1999)

While auteur cinema elevated anime, television massified it on a global scale. In 1986, Dragon Ball premiered, adapting Akira Toriyama's manga. Goku's adventures became Japan's most important cultural export. Sailor Moon (1992) did the same for the mahō shōjo genre, bringing magic and friendship to audiences worldwide, especially female ones.[8]

In 1995, Ghost in the Shell (Mamoru Oshii) topped Billboard's weekly video sales chart in the United States, becoming a symbol of anime "taken seriously" by the West.[9] That same year, Neon Genesis Evangelion (Hideaki Anno) deconstructed the mecha genre and became a study of psychological trauma that continues to be analyzed academically three decades later.

2000 – Today Digital Era

The digital age and streaming dominance

CGI, global phenomenon, and the $25 billion industry

The transition to the 21st century brought the end of physical celluloid. Digital animation software replaced hand‑painted cels, reducing costs and speeding up production. CGI began to be integrated gradually, at first clumsily and then with increasingly refined results. Studios retained the aesthetics of traditional anime while adopting modern tools.

The arrival of streaming platforms such as Crunchyroll (founded in 2006), and later Netflix and Amazon Prime Video, eliminated geographical barriers and distribution delays. The simulcast model —simultaneous broadcast with the original Japanese transmission— allowed fans worldwide to watch new episodes just hours after their premiere for the first time.[8]

Titles that redefined the industry

2004
Fullmetal Alchemist / Brotherhood

Combined action, philosophy, and critique of totalitarianism. Brotherhood (2009) became the highest‑rated series by the global anime community for years.

2013
Attack on Titan (Shingeki no Kyojin)

Demonstrated that adult‑oriented anime could be a global viral phenomenon. It broke audience records and sparked cultural debates in more than 190 countries.

2016
Demon Slayer (Kimetsu no Yaiba)

The film Mugen Train (2020) became the highest‑grossing film in Japanese history, surpassing even Spirited Away.

2015
My Hero Academia (Boku no Hero Academia)

Revitalized the superhero shōnen genre and became one of the most‑watched anime on streaming platforms in Latin America.

Anime today: figures of a global industry

According to 2023 data, the anime industry moves more than $25 billion USD annually worldwide, including licensing, merchandising, streaming, and live events. It is the third most‑viewed type of content on streaming platforms globally, with active fandoms in more than 190 countries. Japan currently produces over 200 anime series per year, with more than 700 registered animation studios.[8]
